# 4 Foot Farm Blueprint: what to check before buying the $7 guide

4 Foot Farm Blueprint is worth considering if you want an organized starting point for a small edible garden. First, check whether your space, schedule, and materials budget suit the project you have in mind. A low-priced guide can help organize decisions, but it cannot supply the sunlight, equipment, or time a garden needs.

Our recommendation is to begin with a simple site checklist and free Extension guidance. Consider paying for the collection if its current plan descriptions answer a question you still have, or if you prefer following one organized resource.

## Check the space before choosing the plan

Use one sheet of paper to describe the proposed growing area. Give it a name, such as “patio beside the kitchen,” and record measurements. Include the parts that must stay clear for doors, seating, storage, or access. A space that looks empty in a photograph may already do several jobs for your household.

Then make a short observation log. On a day when you can check periodically, note when direct sunlight reaches and leaves the proposed location. Record the date and any obstruction, rather than labeling the whole balcony “sunny.” Treat that as a starting observation to compare with seasonal and local growing advice.

University of Maryland Extension recommends **six to eight hours of direct sun for warm-season crops** such as tomatoes and peppers, and **three to five hours for cool-season crops** such as lettuce and spinach. It also emphasizes access to water: some containers need daily watering in hot, dry weather. [Read its container-gardening guidance](https://extension.umd.edu/resource/growing-vegetables-containers-and-salad-tables).

Before shopping, answer these practical questions:

- **Light:** What did I observe, and does it match the requirements of the crop I am considering?
- **Space:** What area can remain dedicated to growing, including room to reach the plants?
- **Water:** Where will I fill a watering can or connect an appropriate water supply?
- **Routine:** Who will check the plants during a busy week or while I am away?
- **Permission:** Do I need to confirm building or rental rules before adding containers or supports?

Keep unanswered items visible. Buying a plan is a separate decision from resolving them.

## Start with foods you actually want to use

Make a list of three vegetables or herbs that appear regularly in your meals. Beside each, write how you use it and whether you would enjoy looking after a plant that produces it. A familiar ingredient gives your project a clearer purpose than a photograph of a densely planted patio.

If you want help spotting those repeat ingredients, our [three-dinner planning guide](/posts/plan-three-plant-based-dinners) offers a simple way to compare what you cook and buy.

Next, investigate the exact crop and variety. Compare its growing requirements with your site notes and regional advice. Do this before ordering a mixed seed collection or selecting a large planter. If none of the three suits the location, revisit the crop list rather than buying supplies for an unsuitable plan.

Set a modest first goal, such as learning to maintain one suitable container. You do not need to frame the purchase around replacing your grocery shopping or producing a particular harvest.

## Build a materials budget around one setup

Make a shopping list for one proposed setup before multiplying it across the patio. Use current local or delivered prices and include the whole purchase quantity: a small project may still require buying a complete bag of growing mix or a full seed packet.

Maryland Extension advises matching container depth and volume to the crop, providing adequate drainage, and using suitable growing media. It warns that filled containers can be much heavier than they look when empty. Its guidance also covers food-safe container selection and avoiding vessels that previously held chemicals. These are useful criteria when evaluating either a paid plan or a store display. [Check the container-selection sections](https://extension.umd.edu/resource/growing-vegetables-containers-and-salad-tables).

Organize your budget into four groups:

- **The growing setup:** the selected container, appropriate growing mix, seeds or plants, and anything the chosen crop requires.
- **Care and access:** items needed to water, maintain, and reach the setup comfortably.
- **Learning:** the guide, if you choose it, plus any other paid instruction you actually intend to use.
- **Later purchases:** decorative upgrades, extra containers, or additional projects you have not committed to.

Mark which items you already own, which need a suitability check, and which must be purchased. Keep the later purchases outside the first order.

If the total is uncomfortable, compare a smaller first project with postponing the purchase. Avoid making the budget work by assuming a harvest will reimburse the cost. Enjoyment and learning can be valid reasons to garden without attaching a savings promise.

## Paid plans or free Extension guidance?

Free guidance can answer an immediate question without buying a collection. Try the Maryland resource linked above, then use regional advice for local decisions. A short checklist you assemble yourself may be enough to choose a first project.

The potential reason to pay for 4 Foot Farm Blueprint is **organization and convenience**. You might prefer a selected collection over choosing between several resources. That is a preference worth evaluating, but the public sales description does not establish that the paid material is clearer, more accurate, or more suitable for your location.

Try this comparison before buying:

- Write down the next decision you need to make, such as choosing between two container setups.
- Check whether the free guidance answers it clearly enough for you to proceed.
- Review the paid collection's available descriptions for that same question.
- If the difference is unclear, seek a substantive sample or more detail before deciding.

Someone who needs local planting dates has a different problem from someone who wants a complete learning sequence. Choose the resource that addresses the actual gap.

## Who should consider it?

The collection may suit a beginner who has a plausible growing location, wants written plans, and prefers a single resource to help organize the next steps. It is a less compelling purchase if you already have a suitable plan, only need an answer to one gardening question, or expect the $7 payment to include physical supplies.

Before choosing it, you should be able to name the area you will use, the crop you want to investigate, your approximate materials budget, and the question the guide would help answer. Compare those notes with the current package and terms. A clear match is a better reason to buy than the number of plans or the appeal of the price.
